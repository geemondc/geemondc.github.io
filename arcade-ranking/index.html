How the ranking was decided — your criteria, in this order

  1. Phone-touch fit — one thumb: drag, swipe, tap, or one virtual joystick + at most 2 buttons. Portrait (vertical-monitor) games gain a lot; drag-to-move with autofire turns most vertical shooters into one-thumb games.
  2. Single-file buildability — one 2D canvas playfield, sprite-level graphics, no laserdisc video, no multi-screen cabinet, no true 3D / sprite-scaler / vector hardware that can't be faked with lines and flat sprites.
  3. Fun in short sessions, timeless not novelty — a loop you want to repeat in 2–5 minutes.
  4. Bright colour.

Hard penalties were applied for: steering wheel/pedals, light gun, trackball/spinner with no good touch substitute, twin/rotary joysticks, 5–6-button panels, laserdisc, dual/triple screens, vector + flight yoke, button-mashing, monochrome vector art, and very large content loads (100s of mazes/levels) in a one-file hobby build.

The tags: EITHER = works as a faithful remake or a remix · REMIX = only worth building re-themed/reworked · IP — remix only = licensed-character game (your rule; mechanics still ranked on merit) · ⚑ insect = your rule: Centipede/Millipede may only be remixed with a non-insect re-theme.

The Top 10 — start here

The researcher's first picks, with the full reasoning. These are the ones to talk through first.

#1

Arkanoid

1986

The single best fit on the whole list: the original used a spinner, but a finger dragging the paddle is better than the original control, and it is portrait, bright, and endlessly replayable in 3-minute bursts. One playfield, one ball, breakable bricks and a dozen power-ups is a weekend-sized build in a single file.

Either
#2

Galaga

1981

The definitive one-thumb shooter — drag to move, autofire, no buttons at all. The tractor-beam capture-and-rescue loop (risk a ship to win double firepower) is a genuine decision, not just reflexes, which is why it still feels modern. Formations, challenge stages and dive patterns are all simple sprites and sine paths.

Either
#3

Pang

1989

Criminally under-remade: one joystick and one button, 50 self-contained stages, and the split-the-balloon mechanic creates natural escalation as every hit makes the problem smaller and faster. The world-landmark backdrops give bright variety for free, and a stage lasts about a minute.

Either
#4

Frogger

1981

Swipe-up/down/left/right to hop — Frogger was designed for touch forty years early. Two half-screens (road, river), five home bays, a bonus fly and a timer: the entire game is a few dozen lines of lane logic, yet it has never stopped being tense. It is also the most instantly understood game here by non-gamers.

Either
#5

Centipede

1981

Drag-to-position plus autofire reproduces the trackball more faithfully than any joystick port ever did, and the segmented, splitting enemy remains a brilliant mechanic. Per your rule, remix it: a conga line of pool inflatables, a string of sausages on a barbecue, anything but bugs — the mechanic survives any skin.

Either⚑ Non-insect re-theme only
#6

Qix

1981

Drawing territory with a swipe is the most "mobile-native" mechanic of 1981 — it is the ancestor of a whole genre of phone games. The risk calculus (slow draw = double points, one touch on your line = death) produces constant near-miss drama, and the fill-in rendering is trivially pretty in canvas.

Either
#7

Klax

1989

A falling-tile puzzler that is not Tetris (which you have shelved): drag the paddle, tap to drop, make lines of three. Bright solid colours, 100 short waves with rotating goals, and a built-in "one more wave" pull. It also diversifies the portfolio — everything else in this top 10 is action.

Either
#8

Pac-Man

1980

On pure merit nothing beats it for buildability (one maze, four ghost personalities) and swipe steering works perfectly. The IP tag means the build should be a remix — new chaser characters and a new maze theme on the same chase/fruit/turn-the-tables skeleton — but the skeleton is the most proven in arcade history.

RemixIP — remix only
#9

Bubble Bobble

1986

The most charming two-button platformer of the decade: blow bubble, trap, pop, collect fruit. Single-screen stages mean each level is a small data table, so a 25–30 stage version is realistic in one file where the full 100 is not. Bright, funny, and the bubble-riding physics gives it depth beyond its looks.

Either
#10

Dig Dug

1982

Digging your own maze is still a unique verb in games, and drag-to-dig maps cleanly to a virtual stick. Two kill methods (inflate, or tunnel under a rock) plus vegetables-for-points gives short sessions a scoring texture most 1982 games lack. It just edges out Rainbow Islands and Rod Land, which are the first two alternates if a cuter platformer is wanted sooner.

Either

First alternates, in order: Rainbow Islands (#11), Rod Land (#12), Galaga '88 (#13), 1943 (#14), Mr. Do! (#15).

Honest notes & open questions

What the research could not verify, and the hedges it attached — carried here exactly as reported.

Could not verify

  • Orbit Blaster (#223) — no arcade game of this exact name could be found in KLOV/Museum of the Game or Wikipedia arcade lists (searched 2026-10-21); only unrelated modern mobile/Flash games use the name. Ranked last on that basis — if you have a year/manufacturer, it should be re-ranked.
  • Years are release years — a few titles straddle years by territory (e.g. Chack'n Pop: 1983 copyright, April 1984 Japanese release — listed as 1984; Xevious: Dec 1982 Japan — listed as 1982; Paperboy: produced 1984, released 1985 — listed as 1985). These ±1-year cases do not affect any ranking position.
  • Cobra Command is ranked as the 1984 Data East laserdisc game (per your no-laserdisc criterion); a different, unrelated 1988 Data East scrolling shooter shares the name — if you meant that one, it would sit in the Middle tier as an ordinary horizontal shmup.
  • U.N. Squadron is tagged IP on the basis of its Area 88 manga licence; if you only mean film/cartoon character licences, treat it as EITHER at the same rank (#96).
  • Orientation/control facts are stated in the per-game lines only where they drove the ranking (e.g. spinner, twin-stick, laserdisc, multi-screen); full per-game orientation tables were not compiled for all 223 titles.
